Myth 10 · 2 min read

"Bitcoin wastes energy."

By Banitama · Reviewed by Ricky Saif · Updated October 21, 2026

Example draft. This page shows the planned format. The author will replace it with their own words and experience before it is final.
The short answer

Bitcoin mining uses a lot of electricity by design, because that cost is what secures the network. Whether it is waste depends on the energy source and on what you compare it with, and both are worth looking at with real numbers.

What people are really asking

Boards and employees ask about environmental impact. Some will raise it as a sincere concern, and some will use it to end the conversation. Either way, the question deserves a straight answer.

What is true in the myth

Bitcoin mining consumes a lot of electricity. The network is designed so that adding blocks costs real energy, and that cost is what makes the ledger expensive to attack. If the mining is powered by fossil fuels, there are emissions. Pretending otherwise would not help a company that has sustainability commitments or reporting obligations.

Where the myth breaks down

“Waste” assumes the energy produces nothing. The energy buys security for a network that settles value without a central operator. Whether that is worth it is a judgement. A fair comparison looks at what the alternatives consume: the banking system, gold mining and the physical infrastructure of payments also use energy, though they are harder to measure and compare.

Three facts shape the discussion:

  • Source matters more than total. Miners look for the cheapest power, and some of the cheapest is stranded or surplus, such as hydropower with no nearby buyers or gas that would otherwise be flared. Estimates of the renewable share vary, so use a recent independent source and state its date.
  • Mining is mobile and price-sensitive. It moves toward cheap energy, which can help balance grids, and away from expensive energy.
  • Holding is not mining. A company that buys and holds Bitcoin does not run mining machines, but it can still ask what its exposure means for its reporting.

How to handle it with your stakeholders

Do not dismiss the concern and do not oversell the counter-argument. State what is known, cite the latest data, say where estimates disagree and explain your own position. If your company has emissions targets, discuss the question with your sustainability team before the board does.
