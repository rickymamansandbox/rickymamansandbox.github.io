Myth 3 · 2 min read

"Our board will never approve this."

By Ricky Saif · Reviewed by Faizal Abdillah · Updated October 21, 2026

Example draft. This page shows the planned format. The author will replace it with their own words and experience before it is final.
The short answer

Boards do not reject Bitcoin; they reject unclear risk. A short decision paper with limits, custody, accounting and an exit rule answers most of what a board asks.

What people are really asking

Behind “the board will never approve it” is usually a quieter worry: the executive does not want to propose something and look foolish. The board’s reputation, and theirs, feels at stake.

What is true in the myth

A board that is shown only enthusiasm will say no, and it should. Directors are accountable for company assets. They are right to be careful with anything volatile, anything new and anything they cannot explain to shareholders or regulators.

Where the myth breaks down

Boards rarely refuse a clear proposal with limits. They refuse vagueness. The same questions come up whenever a board looks at this:

  • How much, as a share of our reserves, and why that amount?
  • What is the worst case, and can we survive it?
  • Who holds it, and what happens if a person or a provider fails?
  • How is it accounted for and audited?
  • Is it allowed where we operate?
  • Under what conditions do we stop or sell?

A decision paper that answers these six questions in plain language is a different conversation from “should we buy Bitcoin?”. It becomes “should we approve this policy?”, which is something boards do every quarter.

A better question

Do not ask the board for permission to be bold. Ask for approval of a small, capped, reversible step with named owners and a review date. A pilot sized so that the worst case is tolerable is far easier to approve than a position.
